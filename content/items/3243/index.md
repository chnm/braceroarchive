        It took some time to earn an M.A. in English.  Forty-five years, maybe – if you’ll accept that my education started as a dream, of my dad, well before I was born.<br/>
        <br/>
        In my wallet I carry a black and white photograph of us.  The snapshot, taken after a rain, captures a dreary day in Castanos, Coahuila, Mejico, 1952.  As I stand on a table of rough wood, wearing a baggy diaper, our white adobe home behind us, the picture speaks to me of his vision.<br/>
        <br/>
        In 1945, encouraged by the Mexico-U.S. Bracero Program, dad left home to change our lives.  It would not be easy.  Trials awaited him.  In his first test, needing a Mexican labor contractor to hire him, my dad failed.  The enganchador judged his hands too soft for the needs of American farmers.<br/>
        <br/>
        This did not deter him.  Despite the dangers, without knowing English, he pursued his vision anyway.  Sixteen years later, on a snowy February 22, 1960, in a letter signed by former U.S. Senator Everett McKinley Dirksen, America officially greeted Ireneo Gomez Saldana.<br/>
        <br/>
        In our photo, as he stands behind me, his right hand cupping my waist, I see my dad in the maturity of his manhood.  He is lean.  He is strong.  In his wry, diminutive smile I see the joy he would’ve expressed had he lived to know I graduated. <br/>
        <br/>
        He valued education.<br/>
        <br/>
        He built our lives on it.  <br/>
        <br/>
        I recall one wintry night, in Chicago, how my mother nervously thread about him.  Occasionally she wrung her hands.  My three sisters, older and smarter than me, remained in their room – as my dad, quietly weeping at our dining room table, tried to still his fears.<br/>
        <br/>
        Propped on elbows, his head held in his hands, my dad stared at a book lying open before him.  It was a trade manual.  The first of many that he would need to study, the book was part of a correspondence course on radio and television repair.  Injured, having lost his job as a punch press operator with the Burton-Dixie Corporation, my dad sought a new job.  <br/>
        <br/>
        I asked my mother why he cried.<br/>
        <br/>
        She said he couldn’t read English.<br/>
        <br/>
        It’s the only time I ever saw him weep. <br/>
        <br/>
        On December 11, 1990, while sitting on the steps of Flowers Hall at Southwest Texas State University, on a rosy winter day I celebrated my graduation with him.  <br/>
        <br/>
        I looked at our photo.  <br/>
        <br/>
        In the way that he holds me – facing out, as if presenting me to the future – I couldn’t help wonder if he wasn’t showing me to myself, suspecting, perhaps, that I’d see this moment one day, and I would recognize just how much he had laid out for me. <br/>
        <br/>
        I do.<br/>
        <br/>
        I did.<br/>
        <br/>
        As I gazed across the campus, knowing that I’d benefited from his dream, the university’s tower clock happened to toll.  It was three.  And it was special.  In the bells’ resonance – traveling across space and time, I imagined – I believed that he heard the same victorious chime.  And it felt good.  I understood what he'd given me.  I’d inherited an American story.<br/>
        <br/>
